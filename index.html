Build this first — it covers five of six schools

A set of roughly twenty pieces built around seven categories covers almost every requirement below. Everything school-specific is an addition to this set, not a separate portfolio.

  1. Life drawing from observation — 5 to 6 piecesStanding and seated poses, quick gesture studies, clothed and un-clothed. Wanted everywhere.
  2. Object / still-life drawing — 2 to 3 piecesDifferent objects, different viewpoints, real light. OCAD, Seneca, MTM, Sheridan all ask for this directly.
  3. Perspective drawing — 2 pieces, one interior, one exteriorTwo-point perspective, a real or imagined room and a real or imagined exterior.
  4. An original character, fully developed — the centrepieceFront / ¾ / profile / back rotation, props, a clear silhouette. This is the single highest-value piece for "character design" — give it the most hours.
  5. A storyboard or short comic sequence8–12 panels telling one small story, ideally starring the character above.
  6. A real sketchbook, kept messy on purposeEvery school wants to see actual process pages, not a cleaned-up imitation of one. Start filling it now if it isn't already underway.
  7. 4–6 personal pieces, any mediumPaintings, digital art, a comic page, a demo reel clip, photography — whatever shows range beyond the assignments above.

"Made in" vs. "Submitted via" — why those are two different columns

"Format" used to mean two different things at once: what the artwork is physically made of, and how it reaches the school. Split apart, the pattern is simple — almost everything can be made in pencil, paint, clay, or a tablet, your choice, with a couple of named exceptions (Sheridan's four observational drawings and its Drawing Test must be done traditionally; Centennial expects real software fluency in about five pieces). But submission is digital everywhere. Nobody on this list accepts a mailed-in physical portfolio. The one partial exception is Centennial, which wants a digital copy ahead of time and a physical copy in hand at the actual Admission Session — because that's a real meeting, not a drop-off.

So if a piece is made on paper or in clay: you photograph or scan it, once, well — flat, even light, no glare, cropped tight — and that photo or scan is the submission. A piece made digitally in Procreate or Photoshop just gets exported. Either way, what lands in the portal is a file.

One piece, six applications

Because every school takes files, not objects, the same piece can go everywhere that asks for its category. Make the life drawing once, scan it once at good resolution, and that single image file gets uploaded to Seneca's SlideRoom, OCAD's SlideRoom, Sheridan's portal, MTM's email, and Centennial's PDF — no re-drawing, no re-shipping. Two things actually change per school, not the artwork itself:

  • File specsCheck each portal's accepted formats and size limits before the deadline — OCAD names exact formats (jpg/png/gif/mp4/PDF); Seneca and Sheridan both want everything compiled into one PDF rather than separate images.
  • Medium restrictionsDon't send Sheridan a digital version of a piece it specifically wants traditional, and keep Centennial's software-made pieces actually made in that software — otherwise the same files travel to every school on the list.
A sketchbook that can actually be uploaded

"Keep a messy, real sketchbook" and "submit everything as a file" aren't in tension — there are two legitimate ways to get there:

  • Paper sketchbook, scanned after the factDraw normally. When it's time to submit, photograph each page flat under even light (daylight from a window beats a lamp) or run it through a flatbed scanner, then merge the images into one PDF in page order. A phone scanning app — Adobe Scan, the iPhone Notes scanner, CamScanner — straightens and crops each page automatically; it's faster than it sounds.
  • A sketchbook that's digital from the startDraw directly in an app on a tablet — Procreate, Clip Studio Paint, Photoshop, or the free option Krita. Every page is already a file; export each as a JPG or PNG at a consistent size and drop them into one PDF.

No school on this list requires the sketchbook to be physical. What reviewers are actually checking for is whether the pages read as real, unforced process — thumbnails, false starts, studies — not whether it was made with a pencil or a stylus.
